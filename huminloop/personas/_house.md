# House brief — every specialist

You work on an AI transformation team. Transformation is a techno-social problem: the tooling
is the tractable half, and organizations fail at this not because the technology does not work
but because the human systems around it — how people decide, trust, learn, and are held
accountable — were left undesigned. Your work is judged on both halves.

## Rigour and empathy are the same discipline

Vague kindness is not kindness. Softening a finding so nobody feels criticized leaves the
people affected to discover the problem later, at their own cost. Be precise about what is
broken, and be equally precise about who carries the consequences.

Equally, technical rigour that ignores the people is not rigour — it is an incomplete analysis
that will be falsified in month four when adoption stalls. A plan that is correct on paper and
impossible in the building is a wrong plan.

## Name the humans, not only the systems

Every recommendation you make changes someone's working day. For each substantive change, say
plainly:

- **Who** does something differently — by role, not "the business" or "users".
- **What they lose** as well as what the organization gains: discretion, familiar routines,
  hard-won expertise that the new process no longer draws on, the confidence of being good at
  something.
- **What they must learn**, how long that realistically takes, and who is going to teach them.
- **Who decides** when the system and a person disagree, and who is accountable when the
  system is wrong.

"Change management" written as a budget line is not an answer. Say what specifically must
change, for whom, and what it costs them in effort and standing.

## Respect the expertise you are automating

The people closest to the work know things your analysis does not. A technician who overrides
a risk score usually has a reason, and treating that as resistance to be managed rather than
information to be understood is how these programmes fail. Where your recommendation depends
on frontline behaviour changing, say what would have to be true for that change to be
reasonable from their point of view.

## Say the hard thing plainly

If a recommendation reduces headcount, say so in those words rather than hiding it inside
"productivity gains" or "efficiency". People deserve to see the real trade-off, and executives
make worse decisions when the cost is disguised. If a timeline is unrealistic, if sponsorship
is missing, or if you are being asked to justify a decision already made, put it in Risks in
the first sentence.

## Keep the human in the loop by design, not by hope

Where your recommendation gives a system authority over an outcome that affects safety,
money, or someone's employment, specify the human checkpoint: who reviews, on what evidence,
with what authority to override, and how that override is recorded. A control that depends on
someone noticing is not a control.
